# What does a property manager do for a Columbus rental owner?

A property manager runs the day-to-day business of a rental on the owner's behalf. In practice that means finding and screening tenants, collecting rent and deposits, arranging repairs, and reporting the money back to the owner. Which of those tasks are included, and which are billed separately, differs from company to company, so the management agreement matters more than the job title.

*General information, not legal advice. Last reviewed 2026-11-12.*

## The core duties

The Ohio Department of Commerce publishes a checklist for owners hiring a manager. Read together, its questions describe the usual scope of the work: screening tenants, collecting rent and security deposits, arranging and paying for repairs, filling vacancies, tracking expenses, and reporting finances to the owner ([Ohio Department of Commerce landlord guide](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/real-estate-and-professional-licensing/consumers/landlords-property-management-guide)).

Grouped by stage of a tenancy:

**Before a tenant moves in**
- Setting a rent and advertising the unit
- Showing the home and taking applications
- Screening applicants
- Preparing the lease and collecting the first payments

**During the tenancy**
- Collecting rent and passing it to the owner
- Handling maintenance requests and paying vendors
- Keeping records of income and expenses
- Sending the owner regular statements

**At the end of a tenancy**
- Handling move-out and the security deposit
- Preparing the unit for the next tenant
- Offering a renewal or starting the leasing process again

Not every company does all of this. Some offer a lower-priced plan that covers rent collection and accounting but leaves leasing to the owner, and some charge extra for items such as lease renewals. Our fee review shows this pattern. Of the nine Columbus-area companies whose own websites publish fees, eight list a separate leasing fee and seven list a renewal fee ([company pricing pages, captured 2026-11-11](#sources)). That does not tell you what a given company includes, only that these are commonly priced as their own line items. See [leasing fees versus renewal fees](/guides/leasing-fee-vs-renewal-fee/) for how they differ.

## What it usually does not include

Be careful with assumptions. Items owners sometimes expect to be covered but should confirm in writing:

- Major repair projects and renovation oversight
- Eviction filings and court appearances
- Property tax, insurance, and mortgage payments
- Short-term rental operations, which are priced on a different basis

The Commerce guide also asks owners to confirm in the agreement how repairs are approved and paid, so that costs do not surprise you ([Ohio Department of Commerce landlord guide](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/real-estate-and-professional-licensing/consumers/landlords-property-management-guide)).

## Handling your money

Rent and deposits pass through the manager's hands, which is why owners are encouraged to ask whether funds are held in a separate trust account, how they are tracked, and how they are reported. The same guide lists warning signs, including repeated late remittances, missing invoices for expenses, and failure to disclose an ownership interest in a repair company used on your property ([Ohio Department of Commerce landlord guide](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/real-estate-and-professional-licensing/consumers/landlords-property-management-guide)).

## Who is allowed to do this work in Ohio

Ohio does not have a separate "property manager" license. According to the Division of Real Estate, managing rental property for someone else for a fee generally requires an Ohio real estate license, and a salesperson works under a supervising broker. Exceptions include owners managing their own property and certain court-appointed or employee arrangements ([Ohio Department of Commerce, property management](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/real-estate-and-professional-licensing/salespersons-and-brokers/guides-and-resources/property-management)). The Division also says whether a license is needed depends on the facts, and lists activities such as negotiating lease terms, setting rent, approving applications, advertising for tenants and collecting rent for an owner as ones that usually bring the work within the license law ([Ohio Department of Commerce, rental requirements](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/real-estate-and-professional-licensing/salespersons-and-brokers/guides-and-resources/rental-requirements)).

The underlying definition of a real estate broker is in [Ohio Revised Code 4735.01](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-4735.01). It covers operating, managing or renting buildings for the public when done for another person for compensation, and it excludes people acting only as a custodian, caretaker or janitor.

If you own the property yourself and manage it yourself, the license law is not aimed at you. See [how to choose a property manager in Columbus](/guides/how-to-choose-a-property-manager-columbus/) for how to check a company's license.

## What to check before you hire

- Which of the duties above are in the base fee, and which are extra
- Whether the fee list names a leasing fee, a renewal fee, and any setup or other charges
- Who approves repairs and above what dollar amount
- How often you receive statements, and what they show
- Whether rent and deposits sit in a separate trust account
- How the agreement can be ended
